# English patterns

Look for clusters, not single hits. One "however" or one short punchy sentence is ordinary writing.

### Content patterns

1. Significance inflation. "serves as a testament", "pivotal moment", "reflects broader trends", "setting the stage for", "indelible mark". Cut the claim about importance and state what happened.
   "was established in 1989, marking a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional statistics" → "was established in 1989 to collect and publish regional statistics"
2. Notability name-dropping. Lists of outlets or follower counts with no context. Cite one specific source and what it said, or drop it.
3. Superficial -ing tails. "..., highlighting/ensuring/reflecting/showcasing/fostering ..." tacked on for fake depth. Cut the tail or turn it into a real clause with a source.
4. Promotional language. "nestled", "vibrant", "rich heritage", "breathtaking", "boasts", "renowned", "must-visit". Replace with neutral, concrete facts.
5. Vague attributions. "Experts argue", "Observers have noted", "Industry reports suggest". Name the source if the text gives one; otherwise keep the claim and ask (don't invent a source).
6. Formulaic "Challenges and future prospects". "Despite its ..., faces several challenges ... Despite these challenges, continues to thrive." Keep only the specific problems and facts.

### Language and grammar

7. AI vocabulary. additionally, align with, crucial, delve, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (verb), interplay, intricate, key (adj.), landscape (abstract), pivotal, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore, valuable, vibrant. These co-occur; use the plain word.
8. Copula avoidance. "serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "features" where "is" or "has" works. Use is/are/has.
9. Negative parallelism. "It's not just X, it's Y", "not merely a song, but a statement", and tailing fragments like "..., no guessing." State Y directly, or write the negation as a real clause ("without forcing the user to guess"). Keep a negation that corrects a likely misreading.
10. Rule of three. Ideas forced into triplets to sound complete ("innovation, inspiration, and insights"). Keep the items that have content; drop the filler ones.
11. Synonym cycling. "the protagonist ... the main character ... the central figure ... the hero". Repeat the noun or use a pronoun.
12. False ranges. "from the Big Bang to the cosmic web" where X and Y aren't on one scale. List the topics.
13. Passive voice and subjectless fragments. "No configuration file needed." Name the actor when the text says who it is: "You don't need a configuration file."

### Style

14. Em and en dashes. Cut them all (U+2014, U+2013), spaced or not, and ` -- ` used the same way. Replace with a period, comma, colon, or parentheses, or restructure. Scan the final text for both characters before returning it.
15. Bold overuse. Mechanical bolding of terms. Unbold all but the one or two genuinely important claims.
16. Inline-header lists. "- **Performance:** Performance has been enhanced through ..." where the label just repeats the sentence. Fold into prose or a plain list. Keep label/value lists (Owner: Ana, Due: Friday).
17. Title Case headings. Use sentence case.
18. Emojis decorating headings or bullets. Remove.
19. Curly quotes in plain text or Markdown. Use straight quotes. Ignore them in text from Word, Docs, or a CMS, which curl automatically.

### Communication

20. Chatbot artifacts. "I hope this helps", "Certainly!", "Let me know if you'd like ...", "Here is an overview of ...". Delete; start with the content.
21. Cutoff disclaimers and gap-filling. "While details are limited ...", "maintains a low profile", "likely grew up ...". Say what isn't known or cut it. Never dress a guess up as fact.
22. Sycophancy. "Great question!", "You're absolutely right". Delete.

### Filler and hedging

23. Filler phrases. "in order to" → "to"; "due to the fact that" → "because"; "at this point in time" → "now"; "has the ability to" → "can"; "it is important to note that" → cut.
24. Stacked hedges. "could potentially possibly be argued that ... might" → one hedge at the original strength ("may"). Don't remove the hedge.
25. Generic upbeat endings. "The future looks bright", "exciting times lie ahead". Cut, or end on the last concrete fact the text already has.
26. Hyphen pairs in predicate position. "the report is high-quality" → "the report is high quality". Keep the hyphen before a noun ("a high-quality report").
27. Authority tropes. "The real question is", "at its core", "what really matters", "fundamentally". Cut the ceremony and state the point.
28. Signposting. "Let's dive in", "here's what you need to know", "now let's look at". Delete; do the thing.
29. Fragmented headers. A heading followed by a one-line restatement ("## Performance / Speed matters."). Delete the restatement.
30. Diff-anchored writing. "This function was added to replace the previous approach ...". Describe the current state, except in changelogs, release notes and migration guides.
31. Manufactured punchlines and staccato. "No aesthetic prior. No nostalgia. The old rules were gone." Join a run of fragments into ordinary sentences. One short sentence for emphasis is fine.
32. Aphorism formulas. "X is the language of Y", "X becomes a trap", "not a tool but a mirror". Replace with the concrete claim it gestures at; if the text never states one, keep it and ask.
33. Fake-candid openers. "Honestly?", "Look,", "Here's the thing", "Real talk" as standalone hooks. Just say the thing. "honestly" mid-sentence is fine.

### What not to flag

None of these is a reliable sign on its own:

- Polished grammar and consistent style. Many writers are professionals or were edited.
- Mixed casual and formal registers, or dry, bland prose without the specific tells above.
- Formal or academic vocabulary outside the list in 7 ("ostensibly", "constituent").
- Letter-style greetings and sign-offs.
- A single transition word ("however", "moreover"), a single em dash in someone else's text, curly quotes alone.
- One short emphatic sentence; "honestly" or "look" mid-sentence.
- Unsourced claims. Most writing is unsourced.
- Clean, complex formatting from templates or editors.
- Watched phrases inside quotations, titles, names, or examples where the phrase is being discussed.

### Signs of human writing (keep these)

- Specific, odd, hard-to-fabricate detail.
- Mixed feelings and unresolved tension.
- Dated slang, references, or in-jokes.
- Varied sentence length.
- Genuine asides, parentheticals, and self-corrections.
- Word choices the writer could defend.
